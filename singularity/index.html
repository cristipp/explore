Singularity Angled Infall

Created on 9 October 2026 by Claude Opus 5.5, prompted by @cristipp127.

What happens to a spacecraft that falls into a black hole at an angle, rather than on a straight radial dive?

Below, a formation of 49 ships falls into a non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole on a slanted path that carries angular momentum. Each ship follows its own exact geodesic, integrated live in proper time, so every deformation you see is computed rather than drawn. Lengths are in units of the hole's mass M, so the picture is the same for any size of hole; only the scale changes.

The radial dive is the familiar textbook case: the body is stretched toward the hole into a needle. The angled fall looks different, and it makes three points:

  1. It is a squeeze more than a scatter. The tidal field stretches along one axis and compresses along the other two, so most of the formation converges rather than flying apart.
  2. The long axis ends up along the path, not pointing at the hole. Ships at slightly different radii orbit at slightly different rates, which shears the fleet into a thread wound around the hole like thread on a spool. A star torn apart by a black hole forms a stream for the same reason.
  3. A ship is kneaded, not just pulled. The stretch axis stays tied to the direction of the hole while the ship swings around it, so the stretch sweeps through the hull. Each structural member cycles between tension and compression, and it fails by fatigue rather than a single clean snap.

The L/M slider sets the angular momentum. Below about 3.645 every ship is captured; just above it the fleet whirls near the edge of capture and splits between ships that plunge and ships that climb back out. How violent any of this is depends on the hole's mass: the tidal force at the horizon falls as 1/M², so a supermassive hole lets a ship cross intact and the damage happens deep inside.

What to look for

  • Most of the fleet closes in on you. The tidal field stretches along one axis and squeezes along two. Ships beside you are pulled toward you, not away.
  • The long axis ends up along the path, not pointing at the hole. Ships at slightly different radii have slightly different orbital periods, and that difference shears the formation out along its own trajectory. This is why debris from a real disrupted star forms a stream wound around the hole.
  • The pinch. Every ship orbits in a plane through the centre, and all those planes cross along a common line. Ships lifted out of the plane are forced back through it — a collision hazard, and the dip in the green curve.
  • Near L/M ≈ 3.64 the fleet splits. Ships whirl at the edge of capture, and arbitrarily small differences in approach decide who plunges and who climbs back out. That is chaotic scattering, and it is the one part of this where the word "scattering" is literally right.
  • Ride any ship you like. Click one in the top view, or use the selector. From a station near the back you watch the fleet pull away ahead of you; from the lead ship you watch it fall behind. Drag the forward view to look around, and double-click it to face front again.

The trajectories are exact, but the rendering is not: light is drawn travelling in straight lines, so there is no lensing, no Einstein ring and no multiple imaging. The black disc is the horizon at r = 2M; the dashed rings mark the photon sphere at 3M and the innermost stable circular orbit at 6M. The fleet is drawn far larger than any real formation would be, so that the deformation is visible at all.
